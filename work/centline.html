Centline

The number a broker quotes is not the number that pays you. Centline exists to show the second one before a driver says yes.

Role
Sole designer and engineer. Research, design system, Flutter app, Supabase backend, and the Cloudflare worker behind the share links.
Constraint
A one-truck owner-operator, often in his first year, reading it one handed at a loading dock at 2am. Not a software person. He will delete anything that wastes his time.
Outcome
Five tabs, 968 tests green, a design system whose contrast lint has caught two failures that looked fine on screen. Nobody who drives for a living has used it yet, which is the honest state of it.
01

The number that bankrupts people

Freight brokers quote a rate in dollars per loaded mile. The empty miles a driver runs to reach the pickup, called deadhead, burn the same fuel and the same maintenance, and they pay nothing.

That gap is not academic. It is the usual reason a new carrier goes under in year one, and it is invisible in the only number anyone quotes. So the whole product reduces to one question: what does this load actually net after the empty miles?

+$260

Looks like a good load. Take it.

What the truck actually does

Rate offered$1,500

Deadhead miles250

Total miles1,250

Cost, all miles$1,550

-$50

The same load. It loses money.

Same truck, same load, same day. A $260 profit and a $50 loss are the same offer seen two ways, and only one of them is real. This case is pinned in the test suite as deadhead_flip_test.dart, where the comment next to the $260 reads: the number that bankrupts people.

02

One rule, and everything downstream of it

Once you accept that the flip is the product, the rule writes itself: every cost figure in the app uses total miles, loaded plus deadhead. Any cost math that touches loadedMiles is treated as a bug until proven otherwise.

That single sentence turned out to be an architectural constraint rather than a style note. It is why arithmetic lives in a pure layer with no I/O and no access to the clock, why money is integer cents and miles are integers, and why a per-mile figure is rounded exactly once, at the moment a real dollar amount is produced. Rounding a per-mile number before multiplying it by a thousand miles is off by dollars, and dollars are enough to flip a verdict.

The decision I keep coming back to

A value the driver has not entered is null, never zero. Zero is an answer. Blank is not. If a required input is missing, the screen says so and refuses to compute, rather than defaulting to something plausible. An app that quietly assumes a maintenance reserve of zero will tell a man a losing load is fine.

03

A design system with one reference

The visual system is a DESIGN.md at the repo root, in the design.md format: tokens in the frontmatter, the reasoning in prose. The whole thing falls out of a single reference, chosen deliberately: a parcel tracking page for a freight load.

A tracking page has one subject and shows it in one state. It puts the status rail at the top, because where is it is the question. It states facts in rows and does not decorate them. It offers the one action that matches the stage, not eleven. You do not enjoy a tracking page. You read it and close it.

Picking that reference did more work than any style guide would have, because of what it rules out for free: marketing language, hero imagery, gradients, glassmorphism, celebratory states, streaks, badges, and any score on a man's safety record. When a new screen is proposed, the question is not whether it looks good. It is whether it follows from the reference.

Every colour literal in the codebase lives in one theme file, so the document and the code cannot drift apart. The document is checked with npx @google/design.md lint DESIGN.md, which validates its structure and its WCAG contrast ratios. It has already caught two failures that looked perfectly fine to me on screen, which is the argument for having a linter rather than an eye.

04

Why the whole look changed

For most of its life the app looked industrial: steel plate, hard shadows, stencilled capitals. It suited a tool used in a truck, and I liked it.

Then the product grew a second half. Alongside the private money math, it gained a public face: the driver's carrier record, sent to brokers and read by strangers who are deciding whether to trust him. On that half, the industrial styling read as a machine's readout rather than as a person's profile, which is exactly the wrong impression when the reader is a broker vetting a new carrier on thin data.

So the system moved to a soft neutral ground, white cards with a hairline edge, generous radii, and sentence-case headings, with one ink-dark panel kept for the answer itself. The look changed because the audience changed, not because I got bored of it.

05

Cutting a tab, then putting it back

The navigation started as five tabs named after nouns: Check, Invoices, Week, Record, Truck. It became four, named after the times of a working life. Then one of them came back, and the reversal is the part worth writing down.

Before, five nouns

  • Check
  • Invoices
  • Week
  • Record
  • Truck

Now, five moments

  • Now
  • Loads
  • Invoices
  • Network
  • Profile

The reasoning: a load is one thread. It is checked, it rolls, it becomes an invoice, it gets paid. Splitting Check and Invoices across two tabs meant two homes for one object, so Money folded into Loads because an invoice is just a load at a later age. The freed slot went to Network, which had been a search box buried three taps deep even though connections are made inside the app.

Losing a tab was the point, not a cost. The front door stayed reserved for the present tense: what is under him right now, and the one action it needs.

Then I put Invoices back. The merge was right on paper and wrong in the hand. Reading a load and billing a load turn out to be different errands in different moods, and folding them together meant invoice controls kept surfacing while somebody was trying to price a load. The reasoning behind the merge has still not been shown wrong, and it is kept beside the code for whoever reads it next, but the argument was settled by using the thing on a phone rather than by being right about it.

06

A bug that broke the promise

The app is offline-first by design, because signal at a dock or in a yard is not a given. The private half of it, costs and verdicts and broker history, never leaves the phone at all.

Then the connected half arrived, and with it a subtle failure. The app asked the auth server whether the driver was signed in. When that service returned errors for about a minute, signed-in drivers were dropped back at the sign-in door. Technically a server blip. In practice, a broken promise: the app said it worked without a connection and then locked a man out because of one.

The fix was to stop asking. A single local flag records that this phone has an account on it, so the question should the app open at the door is answerable in airplane mode, with no network call in the path.

07

Accessibility, asserted rather than assumed

The use case makes accessibility a functional requirement, not a compliance checkbox. One hand, a phone at arm's length, a dark cab, and a person who is tired. So the guarantees are asserted in tests rather than trusted to review: Android and iOS tap-target guidelines, and a labelled-tappable check so every control announces itself, both run on every change.

Contrast is handled at the system level instead of per screen. Because every colour is a token in one document, linting that document checks the palette once rather than auditing screens forever. The two failures it caught were both combinations I had looked at directly and approved.

08

What is not done

No working driver has used this. Every design decision here is reasoned from domain research and from interviews I have not finished, which means the riskiest assumptions are still assumptions. Five questions about the cost model remain open, and two of them can still move the arithmetic: whether maintenance should be reserved per mile or per month, and whether detention should count before it has actually been earned.

Invoices go out as text to paste into an email rather than as a PDF, because a rendering library is not what stops an owner-operator getting paid. The app icon is a placeholder I drew. Those are known, deliberate, and written down rather than discovered later.
