My own app, 2026, in testing

Centline

An invoicing app for truck drivers who own one truck: the load they're on, one button for the next step, and an invoice that can't leave without its paperwork.

For an owner-operator, driving is only half the job. The other half is paperwork, and the money waits on it. A broker (the company that hired him for the load) won't pay until the signed bill of lading and the rate confirmation arrive with the invoice. One missing page means a late payment.

01

Before and after

Before

After a delivery he has to gather the signed bill of lading (the receipt for the freight) and the rate confirmation (the agreed price), write an invoice and send it, often from a loading dock at night. My first version of Centline didn't fix that. It tried to do five jobs at once, and a checklist let him tick boxes without attaching anything.

After

The app opens on the load he's on, with one button for the moment: picked up, delivered, write the invoice. The invoice goes out as one PDF packet, and it won't send until both papers are in it.

02

How I worked

  1. Research

    The slow part was after delivery

    My dad has driven a truck for 35 years and owns his own. Watching that work up close, the time didn't go into driving. It went into the stretch between delivering a load and getting paid for it.

  2. Define

    One driver, one truck, one hand on the phone

    The person is an owner-operator, often in his first year, using a phone one-handed at a dock at night. He doesn't open an app to read reports, and he deletes one that wastes his time. So the brief became two jobs: move the load, and get it paid.

  3. Design

    Every change drawn two to four ways first

    Before any code, I draw the options side by side, pick one and write down why. That's 28 design specs so far. Seeing the options together makes the right one obvious faster than describing it.

  4. Build

    From the broker's email to a signed packet

    A rate confirmation shared from his mail lands on the right load, gets signed on the phone, and joins the invoice. I design and build it myself. Claude Code, an AI coding tool, drafts options and checks the work against rules I wrote, and nothing merges until I approve it.

  5. Test

    Rules held by tests, then someone trying to break it

    Automated tests check every rule on every change, including that each screen fits phones from 280 to 466 points wide. Tests only prove what they check, so a separate AI agent, the breaker, tries to make each finished change fail. In one night it found 11 real defects before any of them shipped.

03

The turning point

The fork in the road

Six weeks in, I cut the whole cost side of my own app: pricing, deadhead (the empty miles driven to reach a load), a load check and a broker network.

Why I went this way

The easy path was keeping what I'd already built. But the real pain wasn't pricing, it was getting from delivered to paid. Two jobs done well beats five done halfway, and it took the app from 5 tabs to 4: Now, Loads, Invoices and Profile.

04

The proof

  • 1button on the home screen, always the next step for the load
  • 4steps from the broker's email to a signed invoice packet
  • 0invoices that can be sent without the signed bill of lading
  • 5 → 2jobs the app does, after cutting what wasn't the pain

What it meansFor a driver, the invoice is the paycheck. Designing around the one moment after delivery, and refusing to send an incomplete packet, protects money he has already earned. For the product, a focused app is one a skeptical driver keeps instead of deleting. It's in testing now and not on the App Store yet.
