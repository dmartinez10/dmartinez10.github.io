Sollo · Tokyo · Summer 2026

Nine locales

The readers are foreign residents of Japan. If the app only works well in English, it has failed the people it was built for. Language is not a translation step at the end. It is the constraint the interface is designed against.

Role
UI/UX engineering intern. I authored every new interface string across all nine locales, owned the typeface loading, and wrote the guards that keep a locale from silently regressing.
Constraint
Nine languages including Easy Japanese, across Latin and CJK scripts, on a first-run onboarding flow where a missing font is the reader's first impression.
Outcome
New strings shipped in all nine rather than English with a fallback. I also shipped visibly broken copy to four of them, found it, and wrote a guard narrow enough that people will still trust it.
01

Who the ninth language is for

  • 9locales, Latin and CJK
  • 2of them are Japanese
  • 0strings shipped as English fallback

The list is English, Japanese, Easy Japanese, Korean, Simplified Chinese, Vietnamese, Portuguese, French and Spanish. The one that explains the product is the third.

Easy Japanese is a real register, used by Japanese municipalities precisely because official Japanese is unreadable to most foreign residents. Its presence says what the app is: not a translation layer over government text, but an attempt to make government text comprehensible. A person who reads some Japanese but not bureaucratic Japanese is not served by an English toggle. They are served by Japanese written for them.

Once that is the premise, English-with-a-fallback stops being acceptable as a shipping state. A fallback means a reader in Hanoi taps into a screen and finds a sentence in a language they did not choose, in a product whose entire promise is that it speaks theirs. So every new string I wrote went into all nine before the change was proposed, not after.

02

The defect I shipped

I authored a new string into all nine locales and reported it done. In four of the Latin-script languages I had written it with the diacritics stripped. Not missing, not English: present, and visibly wrong to anyone who reads those languages. It shipped that way.

The failure underneath it is the part worth keeping. I had verified that the keys existed in all nine files, and I reported that as "all nine locales authored." Those are different claims. A key with wrong text in it satisfies every automated check the project had, because the check was counting keys.

What I actually got wrong

Not the typing. The reporting. I described a weaker verification than I had done in stronger words, which is how a defect gets past a person who is paying attention, and it went out to exactly the readers this product exists for.

03

The guard, and why I made it narrower than I could have

The obvious fix is a check that fails when a Latin-script locale contains no accented characters. It would have caught my bug in all four languages. I scoped it to Vietnamese alone, and wrote down why.

French, Spanish and Portuguese have plenty of perfectly correct strings with no accented character in them. Continuer. Cancelar. Run that rule across those three and it flags roughly fifty correct strings. A check that is wrong fifty times is not a check any more; it is a thing people learn to click past, and the day it is right nobody looks.

Vietnamese is different in a way that makes the rule sound. Its orthography uses diacritics densely enough that a string of any length without one is genuinely suspicious. So the guard fires where it is nearly always right and stays quiet where it would nearly always be wrong.

The principle I took from it

A guard's value is not how many bugs it can catch. It is how much people trust it when it fires. Narrow and believed beats broad and ignored, and the reasoning has to live next to the rule or the next person will "improve" it into uselessness.

04

The typeface, and a fallback with no error

Onboarding is the first thing a new reader sees, and it was fetching its typeface from a CDN at runtime. On a slow connection the screens that form the first impression render in something that is not the brand face. I bundled the three weights the flow uses, about 469KB, so they are present before the app opens.

The follow-up asked me to do the same for the Japanese face. I could not, and the reason is worth more than the task: both CJK weights exceed the repository's one megabyte per file limit, so bundling them is impossible rather than merely undone. I put that reasoning beside the asset entry itself, so the next person to ask "why is Japanese not bundled?" finds the answer where they will look instead of rediscovering it.

The real trap was quieter. The font library matches on family and weight, so a call site asking for a weight that is not bundled silently falls back to a network fetch. No analyzer warning. No runtime error. Nothing to see, and the exact failure I had just spent the work removing, reintroduced by a single future edit.

So I wrote the guard the codebase was missing: a check that scans the onboarding tokens for the weights they ask for and fails when a bundled file is absent. I verified it genuinely guards by deleting one of the font files and watching the test name the exact missing one.

A reviewer then found a real hole in it. One token takes its weight as a parameter, so a scan looking only for literal weights cannot see through it. Rather than document the gap I closed it with a second scan across the call sites, and proved that one by injecting an unbundled weight at a live call site and watching it fail. The test's own comment states what it still does not cover.

05

Cheaper than translating

Two habits saved more work than any of the tooling. The first is reusing keys. When a flow needed a cancel and a sign-out label, both already existed, so the change added no translation work at all. Minting a second key for a word the product had already said would have created nine new strings and a permanent risk that the two drift apart.

The second is checking before writing. Asked to wire an undo into an archive action, I looked for the strings first and found the entire feature already specified and translated in all nine, referenced nowhere. Only the wiring was missing. The work was an afternoon instead of a week, and it cost nothing in translation because the most efficient localization is the string you do not write.

06

What I would do differently

I would render every locale rather than reading it. My mistake survived because I checked files instead of screens. Nine screenshots in nine languages, generated on every change, would have made accent-stripped Vietnamese obvious in a glance, and would also catch the things a text check can never see: a German-length word breaking a button, a CJK line wrapping mid-phrase.

I would say what I verified, not what I intended. "Keys present in nine files" and "authored in nine languages" should never again come out of my mouth as the same sentence.

I never watched anyone read it in a language I do not speak. Every judgment here is mine, and I read two of these nine. The thing this work most needs is not a better guard. It is a Vietnamese speaker with the app open.
