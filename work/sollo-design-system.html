Measuring the spec

A design handoff asked for a colour that could not pass the accessibility gate the same handoff demanded. Being the person who both designs and ships means you find that out before a reader does.

Role
UI/UX engineering intern. I took design handoffs into production Flutter, and owned the design system work: colour, spacing and type tokens, the reusable widget and icon library, and the light and dark theme.
Constraint
A shipped consumer app read by people navigating Japanese government paperwork, in nine languages, on phones down to a 320pt canvas at 130% text.
Outcome
The contested pair went from 2.72:1 to 4.60:1 light and 7.75:1 dark. Two accessibility misses were caught before shipping, one of them mine. Several of my own tests turned out to pass on broken code, which I found by breaking it on purpose.
01

What a handoff cannot tell you

A design handoff arrives as artwork, a couple of browser prototypes, and a written spec. It is a description of an intention. What it cannot contain is what the thing actually measures once it is on a phone, in the app's real theme, next to real text at the size a real person set.

That gap is the job of a designer who also ships the build: not to second guess the designer, but to be where the intention meets the arithmetic. Three times over one summer the arithmetic disagreed, and each time the useful move was to bring a number rather than an opinion.

02

Two things in one spec that could not both be true

I was building a progress indicator for the app's topic drill-down: as a reader taps deeper into a subject, a figure in the background artwork walks a little further along a ridge toward a lighthouse, and the guide at the end of the path closes with a stamp and the trail the reader actually walked.

The spec named a colour for the trail line. It also stated, twice, that this exact pair must be gated and must pass AA. I measured the colour it named.

One step up the same grey ladder the system already had.

Both halves of the spec could not hold, so the question was which one to keep. I kept the gate, because the gate is the promise to the reader and the colour is a preference. Moving one step up a ladder the design system already defined meant the change stayed inside the designer's own palette rather than substituting my taste for theirs, and I wrote the reasoning into the code, the commit and the pull request so the next person finds it where they will look.

A second disagreement in the same handoff went the other way. The stamp's light-mode colours as printed were not what the spec's own formula produced at any value, while its dark-mode pair matched that formula exactly. I shipped the formula, since the document instructs twice to derive from it, and flagged the mismatch back rather than silently picking one. Choosing quietly would have been faster and would have left the designer with a document that no longer describes the app.

03

The arithmetic error, and my test that would have locked it in

Separately, a spec moved the app's "can't find it? ask" escape hatch out of the bottom of the search results and into the header. The reasoning was good and worth keeping: at the bottom of the list, the reader who was not finding what they came for had to scroll past every result they had already rejected before reaching the way out. The people who most need a fallback are exactly the people the list is failing.

The handoff sized the tap target from an assumption that the visible link is 26 points tall. Measured, it is 18. So the target came out at 36 points, under every minimum this codebase enforces and under the 48 the handoff's own checklist requires.

The part I am least proud of and most glad I caught

I had already written a test asserting the height was under 44. It would have passed on the broken value and failed on the correct one, which means my own test would have locked the accessibility miss in place and defended it against anyone who tried to fix it later. The target is now 48 with an 18-point visible link, and the test asserts a floor instead of a ceiling.

A related fix elsewhere is the one I would show a designer. An automated review caught new buttons at 23 points against a 48-point floor. The obvious fix is to make the buttons bigger, and it is wrong: these are quiet captions, and growing them turns a caption into a toolbar. The pills stayed exactly the size the designer drew them and the invisible tap box underneath grew instead. Accessible and unchanged to look at, which is usually the answer when those two seem to be in tension.

04

Testing the tests by breaking the code

After the tap-target episode I stopped trusting a green suite. The habit I picked up is simple: put the bug back, and watch the test go red. If it stays green, the test was never guarding anything.

It caught the contrast test immediately. I reverted the colour to the one the spec asked for, the one measuring 2.72:1, and the contrast test stayed green, because it is a colour-maths file and cannot see a widget. It was checking that a number I had typed satisfied a formula, not that the screen drew in that token. I added a widget-level assertion and confirmed it goes red on the same reverted change.

I broke the code on purpose thirteen times across two pieces of work, and found several of my own tests passing on code that was genuinely broken: one measuring its own test harness rather than the app, one asserting a callout stayed put while a list scrolled on a list too short to scroll, and one reading the frame before the one it meant to check. All now fail on the code they exist to reject.

Why this belongs in a design portfolio

A contrast token, a tap target and a type scale are design decisions that live or die in code. If the test that guards one of them is wrong, the decision silently stops being enforced and everybody keeps believing it. The test is part of the design system, not part of the engineering around it.

05

Nothing is ever cut off

The breadcrumb I built prints the route a reader walked across the top of every page they tap into. On a narrow phone at large text, a long route does not fit, and the two conventional answers are an ellipsis or a sideways scroll. Both were in the prototype. Both hide the words that say where you are, which is the only thing the component exists to do.

So a long route takes a second line, and a single label too long for one line wraps inside its own pill. Verified on the narrowest phone the app supports with text enlarged to 130%, because that is the reader this decision is for.

The same instinct produced a type scale for the app's home. It had a flat 13pt category label sitting under a 20pt wordmark, which is not a hierarchy, it is two sizes. It became a deliberate ladder, and narrow canvases got a second smaller ladder of their own so that a 320pt phone under Display Zoom is not handed sizes it cannot carry.

Sweeping that header across three widths at triple text size surfaced an overflow running 87 points off the side of the smallest supported phone. It was not my widget. I wrote it up as its own failing case rather than absorbing it, because swallowing someone else's bug to keep my own sweep green would have hidden a real defect and quietly weakened the sweep.

06

What I would do differently

I should have measured the spec before I wrote the test, not after. The tap-target sequence only happened in that order because I trusted a number in a document enough to encode it. Measuring first would have cost ten minutes and removed the entire episode.

I raised the contrast disagreement in a pull request, which is late. By then I had built the thing. Bringing the measurement to the designer while the handoff was still warm would have let them choose the replacement colour, which is their call and not mine, rather than reviewing a decision I had already made and documented.

A gate nobody can see is a gate that rots. These checks live in the test suite, so they hold only for people who run it and read it. The version of this I would build now surfaces the contrast ladder in the design tool itself, so the disagreement never has to travel through a pull request at all.
