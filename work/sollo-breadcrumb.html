Sollo · Tokyo · Summer 2026

A record, not a map

A breadcrumb looks like a small component. It is actually a claim about what the reader did, and the moment it makes a claim that is not true, everything else on the page that depends on position starts lying too.

Role
Designer and engineer on the feature. I took the second half of a design handoff into production Flutter and made the navigation decisions inside it.
Constraint
A content tree where the same topic can sit under more than one parent, and readers who arrive from search or a saved link with no route behind them at all.
Outcome
The deepest screens are two taps from anywhere behind them instead of a run of back presses. No new text in any language: the one word it needed was already translated in all nine.
01

A trail that told you where you had been

Sollo's guides sit in a tree. A reader taps Daily Life, then a topic, then a sub-topic, then the guide itself, and the further in they go the harder it is to get back out. What existed was a line at the very bottom of the guide, under the closing stamp, naming the levels they had passed through.

It was accurate and useless. It sat at the end of the thing you had already finished reading, it named where you had been, and it gave you nothing to do about it. The way back was still a run of back presses.

So the trail moved to the top of every page and became the way back: a home button, each level behind you, then the page you are standing on.

02

The decision the whole thing rests on

Tapping a level in that strip has two possible meanings, and they look identical to the person tapping. It can go back to the screen already open behind them, or it can open a fresh copy of that screen on top of everything else. The second is easier to build and it is wrong.

The reason is a feature I had shipped the day before. The artwork behind these pages carries a small figure who walks further along a ridge toward a lighthouse on each level down, so the background itself says how deep you are.

Why the easy version breaks the page

Open a copy and the reader is standing three levels back while the stack behind them is still four deep. The walking figure would still be at the lighthouse. The indicator I had just built would begin lying the instant anyone used the breadcrumb, and it would lie in a way nobody could explain: the picture says you are at the end, the words say you are near the start.

Every level named in that strip is, by definition, already open behind the reader. So the app walks back to the real one rather than manufacturing a new one. A navigation control that adds to the stack while appearing to subtract from it is the kind of bug that produces a support message no one can reproduce.

03

The same topic, twice, on one route

The obvious way to find the screen to return to is to match on its name. That works until it does not: a topic in this tree can sit under more than one parent, so the same name can appear twice on a single route. Match by name and a reader aiming at the second one lands on the first, several levels further back than they asked for, with no way to tell what happened.

Each screen is therefore stamped with two things: its name and how deep it sits. The app looks for the pair. Identity here is not what a screen is called, it is what it is called and where it is.

A second case needed the same treatment. A reader who arrives from search lands partway down a tree they never walked, and they can keep walking from there. Without marking that landing point, one tap downward would have thrown them past their own search results and all the way to the home screen, losing the thing they came from.

04

Refusing to invent a route

A reader can arrive at a guide from search, from Saved, or from a link somebody sent them. In those cases there is no walked route, because they did not walk one.

The middle steps are technically reconstructable. Every guide has a parent, so the app could work upward and print a plausible path. It would look complete, and it would be fiction, because a guide with two parents has no single true route and the app would be picking one and presenting it as history.

The rule I would keep

A breadcrumb is a record of what the reader actually did. Inventing one is worse than showing none, because a component that is right most of the time teaches people to trust it, and then it is wrong at exactly the moment they are lost.

So arriving without a route shows the house, the page, and nothing in between. It is visibly shorter, which is the honest signal: you did not come the long way, and this strip is not pretending you did.

The same instinct runs through the rest of this feature and the design system work beside it, where a long route takes a second line instead of an ellipsis, because a component whose job is to say where you are should never hide the words that say it.

05

What I would do differently

I designed this from the tree, not from the reader. Every decision here is defensible from the data model, and none of it came from watching a person get lost. The duplicate-parent case is real, but I found it by reading the content structure rather than by seeing anyone land in the wrong place.

The empty route deserved a word, not just a gap. Showing the house and the page with nothing between is honest, but a reader who arrives from a shared link sees a strip that looks truncated rather than deliberately short. A quiet label would have said "you arrived here directly" instead of leaving them to infer it.

I never tested it with a screen reader on the duplicate case. Two levels with the same name in one strip are distinguishable by position on screen and identical when read aloud. I know what the fix looks like and I did not build it.
